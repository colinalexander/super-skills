# Market and positioning

## Research for a decision

Begin with the decision the research must support. Define the market boundary, customer, geography, time period, and unit of analysis before collecting numbers. Prefer primary, recent evidence; cite sources and dates. For market sizing, show assumptions and calculate ranges rather than presenting a single unsupported total.

Compare competitors as customer alternatives, including manual work, internal solutions, and doing nothing. Separate observed capabilities and prices from inferred strategy. Treat review sites, search results, and social discussion as biased samples rather than representative truth.

## Segment and position

Choose segments using meaningful differences in problem intensity, buying process, value, willingness to change, and cost to reach—not demographic labels alone. Define:

- the target customer and triggering situation;
- the job or outcome they seek;
- the current alternative and its cost;
- the differentiated promise;
- the evidence that makes the promise credible;
- the category or frame that helps the customer understand the offer.

Pressure-test the position against actual alternatives and customer language. A useful position constrains product, channel, and message choices; a slogan alone does not.

## Preserve a shared context

Maintain one factual commercial brief for audience, problems, alternatives, promise, proof, pricing, voice, and prohibited claims. Mark assumptions and expiration dates. Update it when evidence changes so later assets do not silently diverge.
